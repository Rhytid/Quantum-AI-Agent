Abstract
Quantum key agreement (QKA) is an important cryptographic primitive that plays
a pivotal role in private communications. However, in practical implementations
of QKA, the ﬂaws in participants’ detectors may be exploited to compromise the
security and fairness of the protocol. To address this issue, we propose a two-party
measurement-device-independent QKA protocol, effectively eliminating all detector-
side-channel loopholes. This protocol is based on quantum entanglement swapping
and Bell-state measurements, making it feasible under current technological condi-
tions. A thorough security analysis is conducted, demonstrating its ability to guarantee
both security and fairness.
Keywords Quantum key agreement · Measurement-device-independent ·
Detector-side-channel attack · Quantum entanglement swapping · Bell-state
measurement
1 Introduction
In recent years, securing group communication has garnered signiﬁcant attention
within the academic community. The approach to ensuring secure group commu-
nication revolves around maintaining a conﬁdential key, known exclusively to all
members of the group. This process of key generation is commonly referred to as
group key establishment. There exist two principal methods to achieve this goal. The

ﬁrst is centralized key establishment, which involves a single party generating the

client–server or peer-to-peer scenarios) as well as large multicast groups. However,
in many collaborative group settings such as remote board meetings, teleconferences,
whiteboard sessions, shared instrument control, secure and efﬁcient data sharing, col-
laborative workspaces, cloud computing, and command-and-control systems becomes
imperative. This entails employing distributed group key agreement techniques.
A key agreement protocol seeks to generate a shared conference key for multiple
participants, ensuring the security of subsequent group communications in a manner
where all participants exert inﬂuence on the outcome. Since its inception in the seminal
paper by Difﬁe-Hellman [1], key agreement protocols have evolved into fundamental
cryptographic primitives. However, classical key agreement protocols rely on public
key cryptography, which hinges on computational complexity assumptions for secu-
rity. The emergence of quantum computing has introduced new security threats to
classical cryptosystems, leading to the development of quantum cryptography.
In 1984, Bennett and Brassard introduced the renowned BB84 quantum key distri-
bution (QKD) protocol [2]. Since the inception of QKD, various branches of quantum
cryptography have been developed, encompassing QKD [3, 4], quantum secure direct
communication (QSDC) [ 5–8], quantum secret sharing (QSS) [ 9, 10], quantum pri-
vate comparison (QPC) [ 11–13], quantum signature [ 14–16], quantum private query
(QPQ) [17–24], quantum identity authentication (QIA) [ 25–27], quantum key agree-
ment (QKA) [ 28–48] and anonymous quantum communication [ 49].
In 2004, Zhou et al. [28] introduced an innovative QKA protocol that utilizes quan-
tum teleportation. However, Tsai and Hwang identiﬁed fairness concerns in Zhou
et al.’s [29] protocol, highlighting its susceptibility to participant attacks, which enable
one party to unilaterally determine the agreement key and distribute it to the other
party without detection. Subsequently, a series of novel QKA protocols were proposed
[30–48]. A secure QKA protocol should adhere to the following criteria: (C1) Correct-
ness: At the protocol’s conclusion, all participants should possess the correct agreement
key provided they have executed the protocol honestly. (C2) Fairness: Each partici-
pant should wield an equivalent level of inﬂuence over the agreement key, rendering it
impossible for any non-trivial subset of participants to unilaterally determine it. (C3)
Security: The protocol should be designed such that external eavesdroppers cannot
acquire information about the agreement key without triggering detection mecha-
nisms.
While the security of quantum cryptographic protocols is theoretically guaranteed
by the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics, their real-world implementa-
tions often encounter overlooked vulnerabilities, particularly in the imperfections of
detector devices. Attackers can exploit these vulnerabilities to access agreement key
information by employing quantum hacking techniques such as beam-splitting attacks,
time-shift attacks, and blinding attacks [ 50–52]. Consequently, the burgeoning ﬁeld
of quantum hacking presents a signiﬁcant threat to the practical security of quantum
cryptographic protocols. To address the aforementioned challenges, Acín et al. [ 53]
proposed the concept of Device-Independent QKD (DI-QKD) in 2006. However, the
security of DI-QKD relies on loophole-free Bell tests between distant communica-
tion parties. Additionally, DI-QKD demands detectors with close-to-unit efﬁciency,

and its key generation rate is relatively low, rendering it impractical for real-world

Among all the concerns related to device security, vulnerabilities at the measure-
ment device were historically considered the most challenging issue in the practical
application of quantum cryptography. V arious attacks, including phase remapping
attacks, time-shift attacks, and SPD blinding attacks [50–52], have long stood as signif-
icant barriers to progress in this ﬁeld. In response to these severe measurement device
attacks and to address various shortcomings in DI protocols, researchers have intro-
duced a specialized protocol known as the Measurement-Device-Independent (MDI)
protocol. In 2012, Lo et al. [ 54] ﬁrst introduced MDI-QKD. This protocol necessi-
tates both communicating parties, Alice and Bob, to prepare the required single-qubit
quantum states and transmit them to the untrusted third party, Charlie, for Bell state
measurement. MDI-QKD remains immune to attacks targeting detectors, providing
high key rates and communication distances. It effectively eliminates all detector
side channels by assuming that legitimate participants possess trusted quantum state
preparation devices. The ﬁeld of MDI-QKD has witnessed numerous groundbreak-
ing contributions. For example, the initial implementation of a free-space MDI-QKD
protocol [55] was founded on a practical scheme from the literature [56], signiﬁcantly
surpassing the effective atmospheric thickness and paving the way for satellite-based
MDI-QKD. Furthermore, various novel MDI-QKD protocols, such as twin-ﬁeld QKD
[57–61], mode-pairing QKD [ 62], and MDI-QKD network [ 63, 64], have been pro-
posed.
However, there is a scarcity of research on MDI-QKA. Recently, Cai et al. [ 65]
introduced a three-party MDI-QKA protocol. In this protocol, participant Charlie is
tasked with measuring the Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) state particle ’ c’ that
he possesses, and his Z basis measurement results are employed for key generation.
However, if an external eavesdropper were to target Charlie’s measurement device and
gain control over it, the measurement results could be pilfered by the eavesdropper,
resulting in key leakage and posing a substantial threat to the security of the MDI-QKA
protocol.
To eliminate all detector-side-channel vulnerabilities in QKA, this study presents
an innovative two-party MDI-QKA protocol. This protocol leverages quantum entan-
glement swapping techniques, allowing participants to delegate measurement tasks to
an untrusted third party, thereby eliminating the potential for detector-side-channel
attacks. The proposed protocol delivers signiﬁcantly enhanced practical security com-
pared to existing two-party QKA protocols.
The subsequent sections of this paper follow this structure: Sect. 2 provides
preliminaries, encompassing the characteristics of Bell states and the principles of
quantum entanglement swapping. Section 3 introduces a two-party MDI-QKA pro-
tocol. Section 4 conducts a thorough analysis of the protocol, assessing correctness,
fairness, and security. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion and summary.

4.3 Security
Security means that no external eavesdropper can obtain the information about the
agreement key without being detected. In our protocol, the security of all the photon
transmission processes is guaranteed by the two rounds of security checking. Sup-
pose Eve is an external eavesdropper who attempts to eavesdrop on the agreement
key. Since the decoy photons and the EPR particles are in the maximally mixed state
ρ /equal11
2 (|0⟩⟨0| + |1⟩⟨1|), Eve cannot discriminate the decoy photons and the EPR parti-
cles. Thus, Eve cannot obtain any information only from Alice’s and Bob’s traveling
particles. To see this in a sufﬁcient way, we will consider two possible cases, in which
Eve may execute two common attacks: intercept-resend attack and collective attack.
For the intercept-resend attack, it is unavoidable for Eve to intercept some decoy pho-
tons. As Eve does not know the bases of the decoy photons, she may choose wrong
measurement bases. Obviously, the probability that Eve selects a wrong measurement
basis is approximately 50%. In this way, after she sends the photons to Charlie, Charlie
may obtain a BSM result which may mismatch Alice’s and Bob’s decoy photon states
and increase the error rate of the security checking. As a result, the intercept-resend
attack can be detected by Alice and Bob.

5 Discussionandconclusion
Our work represents more than a mere extension of existing research. Currently,
QSDC/DSQC has been proposed for QKA protocols. However, a majority of the
current QKA protocols remain susceptible to detector-side-channel vulnerabilities.
Remarkably few studies have ventured into MDI-QKA, with only the protocols in
[65, 67] tackling this domain. Nonetheless, even among the existing MDI-QKA stud-
ies [ 65, 67], substantial practical ﬂaws persist. First and foremost, the protocol in
[65] does not qualify as a genuine MDI-QKA protocol, as it remains susceptible to
security attacks. In this protocol, Charlie, a participant in the key agreement process,
is tasked with measuring the GHZ state particle ’c’ using his measurement device
to generate the key based on the measurement result. However, Charlie’s measure-
ment device may be eavesdropped upon or even controlled by an external attacker,
compromising the entire protocol’s security. For an MDI-QKA protocol claiming to
withstand all side-channel vulnerabilities, the existence of such a vulnerability is catas-
trophic. Recently, Liu et al. [ 67] presented a multiparty MDI-QKA protocol. In their
protocol, the post-selected GHZ states are used to generate a secure agreement key
between legitimate participants. However, GHZ-state measurements are required and
this protocol is probabilistic because only two of 2 N N-particle GHZ-state measure-
ment results can be identiﬁed by using the linear optical elements. In contrast, our
two-party MDI-QKA protocol is deterministic. Since multi-party MDI-QKA is more

To summarize, we have presented an innovative two-party MDI-QKA protocol
based on entanglement swapping. Signiﬁcantly, this protocol guarantees that no sub-
set of participants can independently ascertain the ﬁnal shared key. Moreover, we also
show that the proposed protocol is secure against the intercept-resend attack and collec-
tive attack of external attackers, and it is also secure against participant attacks, thereby
ensuring both fairness and security. Furthermore, the generation of Bell states and sin-
gle photons is achievable with current technologies. In addition, our protocols need
complete Bell state analysis. Though Bell state measurement has not been generally
accomplished [69], it was experimentally realized based on some certain techniques
[70–73]. Furthermore, the realizations of entanglement swapping have been proposed
[74]. Therefore, our protocols are within the reach of current technology. Finally, we
hope that the MDI technique should be taken into account in the design of practical
quantum cryptographic protocols [ 75–92].
