Abstract
In this thesis, we explore the applications of the theory of operator
algebras, such as C∗-algebras and von Neumann algebras, in quantum
physics with a focus on the quantum bilocal network scenario. Origi-
nating from Heisenberg’s picture, the theory of operator algebras has
become a fruitful ﬁeld and a powerful mathematical tool in the study of
quantum systems. Quantum nonlocality is essential for understanding
the foundations of quantum mechanics, and quantum correlations in
network scenarios provide a robust framework for its investigation.
We combine the algebraic perspective with the study of quantum non-
locality to analyse the quantum bilocal network scenario, involving
three parties, Alice, Bob, and Charlie, arranged linearly. In this scenario,
two independent quantum sources are present, one shared between
Alice and Bob, and the other shared between Bob and Charlie. Recent
developments from [57, Renou & Xu, arXiv:2210.09065] and [35, Ligthart
& Gross, arXiv:2212.11299] demonstrate the applicability of operator
algebras in this context, offering a deeper understanding of quantum
nonlocality in network settings.
This thesis serves as a reference for results in the theory of operator
algebras relevant to the study of quantum nonlocality, introducing
notable results and attempting to draw connections between them. By
building upon the ﬁndings of the two papers mentioned above, the
thesis highlights the potential of these abstract mathematical tools in
uncovering new insights into quantum mechanics and motivates further
research in the ﬁeld using an operator algebraic perspective.

Chapter 1
Introduction
The study of quantum mechanics has led to the development of two alterna-
tive formulations, namely Schr ¨odinger’s picture and Heisenberg’s picture.
Schr ¨odinger’s picture, introduced by Schr¨odinger in 1926, is based on wave
functions representing quantum states, which evolve with time according
to the Schr ¨odinger equation [59]. In contrast, Heisenberg’s picture, intro-
duced by Heisenberg in 1925, employs the concept of matrices representing
observables, with the quantum states seemingly absent [26].
Von Neumann initially demonstrated the equivalence of these two seemingly
different formulations in 1927 [43, 48]. Building upon the operator algebra
ideas inherent in Heisenberg’s matrix mechanics, von Neumann, in collab-
oration with Murray in the 1930s, deepened the understanding through a
series of works [39, 40, 41, 44, 45, 46, 47, 49], which laid the groundwork for
the theory of operator algebras. This theory was later developed further by
Gelfand, Naimark, and Segal [23, 60].
In 1947, Segal’s paper [61] marked another turning point in the relationship
between operator algebras and mathematical quantum physics, introducing
the concept of C∗-algebras to the ﬁeld. Since then, the theory of operator
algebras has become a powerful tool, ﬁnding applications in quantum sta-
tistical mechanics [11, 24], quantum ﬁeld theory [3, 24], and even giving
rise to the ﬁeld of noncommutative geometry [18] with potential applica-
tions in quantum ﬁeld theory, quantum gravity, and string theory [5, 19, 37,
62, 65]. The algebraic perspective has proven invaluable in describing and
analysing quantum systems, showcasing the profound interplay between
operator algebras and quantum mechanics.
In 1935, the EPR paradox, proposed by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen, ques-
tioned the completeness of quantum mechanics and introduced the concept
of entanglement [20]. Building on the idea of entanglement, in 1964, Bell
introduced the concept of nonlocality by proving Bell’s theorem [7], which
1

showed that quantum mechanics inherently exhibits nonlocal correlations
that cannot be explained by local hidden variable theories. This result re-
vealed that nonlocality is a fundamental feature of quantum mechanics and
has led to a plethora of applications, such as quantum cryptography [1, 2, 6,
71], randomness [17, 51], and quantum device certiﬁcation [4, 36, 66].
In particular, understanding the set of probability distributions feasible by
quantum theory in any given setup is crucial in the study of quantum
nonlocality. In the standard Bell scenario (see, e.g., Figure 3.2 and 4.1), where
multiple parties share one quantum source, one can approximate the set of
feasible quantum correlations from within by considering all possible Hilbert
spaces and operators. On the other hand, the seminal paper by Navascu ´es,
Pironio, and Ac ´ın [42] provides an outer approximation of the quantum
set, introducing the NPA hierarchy—a convergent hierarchy of semi-deﬁnite
programs (SDP) that can be solved by computers [70, 75].
More recently, there has been growing interest in studying network scenarios
involving multiple independent quantum sources [9, 22, 54, 55, 68]. These
scenarios provide new insights into the foundations of quantum mechanics
and can help to understand quantum correlations in more complex set-
tings [15, 16, 27, 67]. The bilocal scenario [10], which is the simplest network
scenario involving three parties sharing two independent quantum sources
(see Figure 4.2), is a prime candidate for investigation [53, 56, 73].
In order to characterise the set of quantum correlations feasible in these more
complex quantum network scenarios, various methods were considered. For
example, the quantum inﬂation technique [74], which involves duplicating
quantum sources in an inﬂated network so that the additional symmetries can
be exploited to characterise network correlations. In the bilocal scenario, the
scalar extension hierarchy is introduced to linearise factorisation constraints
and achieve a tighter approximation than the standard NPA hierarchy [52].
A nonlinear generalisation of the NPA hierarchy is investigated in [57] to
study the bilocal scenario, further formalising the scalar extension hierarchy.
Subsequently, the Positivstellens¨atze of state polynomials are proven, gener-
alising the scalar extension polynomials in [57] and leading to convergent
SDP hierarchies capable of probing network scenarios [32]. In addition to
addressing the open questions posed in [57], the authors of [35] demon-
strate the convergence of the inﬂation-NPA hierarchy in the bilocal scenario.
Throughout these advancements, the algebraic aspects of quantum theory
naturally surface and prove to be essential.
Motivated by these developments, this thesis aims to integrate the algebraic
perspective of quantum mechanics with the study of quantum nonlocality
in quantum network scenarios, establishing a foundation for future research
and uncovering potential connections and insights.

1.1. Thesis outline
1.1 Thesis outline
This thesis is divided into four main chapters and an appendix, providing a
comprehensive treatment of the relevant concepts and techniques in operator
algebras and their application to quantum nonlocality.
In Chapter 2, surveying across [8, 11, 30, 33, 38], we delve into the algebraic
formulation of quantum mechanics, examining the underlying algebraic
structures and their applications. This chapter begins with an introduction to
the theory of C∗-algebras, which models physical observables and provides
a powerful abstraction for quantum mechanics. We then explore (algebraic)
states and representations of C∗-algebras, allowing us to associate abstract
algebras with concrete Hilbert spaces and their bounded operators through
the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS) construction. Following this, we inves-
tigate von Neumann algebras, a more specialised subclass of C∗-algebras
with unique properties that are particularly relevant to quantum theory.
Superselection rules, which are important physical phenomena restricting
feasible observables, are brieﬂy introduced after the von Neumann–Murray
decomposition theorem. Next, composite systems and independent sub-
systems are discussed, where the notion of tensor product of C∗-algebras
and von Neumann algebras are examined. Finally, the chapter concludes by
presenting the algebraic quantum postulates A’–C’ based on Heisenberg’s
picture to contrast the standard quantum postulates A–C.
Chapter 3 provides an overview of quantum correlations, focusing on nonlo-
cal correlations in the context of two-player nonlocal games. We discuss the
geometry of correlation sets [12], the CHSH game [14], and Tsirelson’s prob-
lem [21, 58, 69]. Moreover, we introduce the Navascu ´es-Pironio-Ac´ın (NPA)
hierarchy [42], a powerful technique for analysing quantum correlations and
characterising the set of feasible quantum correlations. This chapter serves as
a foundation for understanding the signiﬁcance and implications of quantum
correlations for the subsequent chapter.
In Chapter 4, we apply the mathematical formalism introduced in previous
chapters to study quantum nonlocality, speciﬁcally focusing on the quantum
bilocal network scenario, which involves a line arrangement of three parties,
namely Alice, Bob, and Charlie, who share two independent quantum sources
as in Figure 4.2. The chapter discusses the factorisation bilocal NPA hierarchy
and its convergence to a relaxation of tensor bilocal quantum correlations [57].
Furthermore, the chapter explores algebraic insights into the quantum bilocal
network, discussing alternative models and their equivalence, as well as
addressing the bilocal Tsirelson’s problem [35]. Although relevant, the
primary focus of the chapter is not on scalar extension [52, 57], inﬂation
technique [34, 35, 74], and Positivstellens ¨atze of state polynomials [32], thus
we only brieﬂy mention these topics in remarks.

In Appendix A, we provide an overview of the basics of functional analysis
based on [8, 30, 33, 38], including Banach spaces and Banach algebras, the

1.2 Contribution and discussion
This thesis serves as a reference for results in the theory of operator algebras
relevant to the study of quantum nonlocality, introducing notable results and
attempting to draw connections between them. While the main contribution
is not the original application of operator algebras to quantum bilocal network
scenarios, it does summarise two recent works [35, 57] in the same notation,
highlighting the signiﬁcance of these works in solving problems in the
bilocal scenario with new insights. The thesis emphasises the value of
algebraic insights in understanding quantum nonlocality and showcases
the usefulness of operator algebras in providing a deeper understanding
of quantum mechanics and its underlying structure. Therefore, the thesis’
contribution is mainly in surveying various publications and making the
connections between these works more explicit.
Potential future research directions include incorporating the existing meth-
ods in further investigation of bilocal quantum scenarios, and the develop-
ment of algebraic models for more general quantum networks and their
implications in quantum foundations. Among many possibilities, there are:
• One of the open problems is the convergence of the scalar extension
technique [52] in the bilocal scenario, as attempted in [57, Appendix C]
without a full resolution. The recent work on Positivstellens ¨atze of state
polynomials [32] seems to provide insights from the primal point of
view, and understanding it in the context of the dual perspective [57]
might be the key.
• The generalisation of the algebraic formulation to more general quan-
tum network scenarios is a promising direction. In particular, one can
consider the quantum inﬂation technique [74], which is a powerful
method for characterising correlations in general network scenarios.
Therefore, it would be interesting to explore the convergence of the
quantum inﬂation-NPA hierarchy to alternative quantum models in
generalised networks.
In summary, this thesis presents a comprehensive exploration of the algebraic
perspective in quantum mechanics and its applications in understanding
quantum correlations and the bilocal network scenario. Serving as a refer-
ence for quantum nonlocality studies, the thesis connects recent works and

Chapter 2
Algebraic structures in quantum
mechanics
In this chapter, we delve into the algebraic formulation of quantum mechanics,
a complementary perspective to the traditional Schr ¨odinger’s picture. By
examining the underlying algebraic structures of quantum mechanics, we
gain insights and discover numerous applications.
Primarily based on [8, 11, 30, 33, 38], we begin with an introduction to
C∗-algebras, which model physical observables. Following this, we explore
(algebraic) states, representations, and the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS)
construction, which associates abstract algebras with concrete Hilbert spaces
and their bounded operators. Subsequently, we discuss von Neumann alge-
bras, a specialized subclass with properties particularly relevant to quantum
theory. The chapter also investigates superselection rules and their impli-
cations for quantum theory, as well as the notion of tensor products in the
algebraic framework, essential for multipartite quantum systems. Finally,
we conclude the chapter by contrasting algebraic quantum postulates with
standard quantum postulates, highlighting their differences and similarities.
To provide context for the forthcoming algebraic deﬁnitions, we present
the standard formulation of postulations in quantum theory, namely the
Schr ¨odinger’s Hilbert space–vector state approach. For the purposes of this
thesis, we will not consider the evolution of quantum systems, thus focusing
on a framework that omits this aspect. The following discussion assumes
ﬁnite-dimensionality for simplicity and draws on the classical reference for
quantum information theory [50].
Deﬁnition 2.1 A density operator (or density matrix) ρ on a Hilbert spaceH is
a positive operator that is normalised. That is, ⟨ϕ|ρ|ψ⟩≥ 0 for any|ϕ⟩ ,|ψ⟩∈H
and Tr(ρ) = 1. The set of all density operators S(H) is called the state space.

2.3 von Neumann algebras
C∗-algebras offer a powerful abstraction in the study of quantum mechanics,
yielding numerous results. However, their limitations call for a more spe-
cialised subclass: von Neumann algebras. While C∗-algebras are closed in
the uniform topology, it is often too restrictive; weak topology arises more
naturally in quantum theory, and von Neumann algebras are both strongly
and weakly closed. Furthermore, unbounded operators, abundant in quan-
tum theory, are challenging to handle within C∗-algebras due to domain
changes induced by adjoint operations. In contrast, von Neumann algebras
can model unbounded operators through associated PVMs. Also, the von
Neumann–Murray factor classiﬁcation provides valuable structural insights.
Finally, the special structure of von Neumann algebras enables concepts such
as superselection rules to be easily modelled.
This section is mainly based on the discussion in [38, Chapter 6].
2.3.1 Deﬁnitions and basic properties
Recall that for a set of operator M ⊂ B(H) of some Hilbert space, its
commutant is denoted by M′ ={ˆα∈B (H)| [ˆα, ˆβ] = 0,∀ ˆβ∈ M}. We begin
by including some basic facts about commutants from [38, Proposition 6.3].
Proposition 2.24 The commutant M′ of M⊂B (H) satisﬁes the following proper-

(a) If M is closed under the involution †, then M′ is a C∗-subalgebra;
(b) M′ is closed in both weak and strong topologies;
(c) M′ = (( M′)′)′, hence no more new information above the second commutant.
As mentioned before, the weak operator topology arises much more naturally
from quantum theory, rooted from the convergence of matrix elements of

2.3.3 Factors and structures of von Neumann algebras
One feature of von Neumann algebras is that they have many projectors
(in fact, they are generated by them) [33, Appendix C.21]. The study of
these projectors leads to the concept of factors which are the “irreducible”
components in von Neumann algebras. Factors are the building blocks of the
von Neumann-Murray decomposition theorem.
Deﬁnition 2.32 A von Neumann algebra M is called a factor if it has a trivial
center, that is, Z(M) = M∩ M′ = C.
Von Neumann and Murry classiﬁed factors based on the properties of their
minimal projectors: type I, type II, and type III. While type I factors are easier
to describe, it would be hard to deﬁne the other two rigorously without a
long setup beyond the scope of this thesis, as they are less relevant to the
elementary quantum theory.
• Type I factors: A von Neumann algebra is a Type I factor if it contains a
minimal projection. These factors can be further divided into Type I n
factors, where n is the dimension of the central projections, while Type
I∞ is inﬁnite-dimensional.
Examples of Type I factors include the algebra of all bounded operators
on a Hilbert space,B(H). Additionally, a factor is of type I if and only
if it is isomorphic to some B(H) [38, Chapter 6.1.4].
• Type II factors: A von Neumann algebra is a Type II factor if it has
no minimal projections but has projections equivalent to the identity.
Type II factors are also divided into Type II 1 and Type II ∞. Type II 1
factors have a unique, normalised, ﬁnite trace, while Type II ∞ factors
have traces that are only semiﬁnite. Type II factors often arise in the
context of inﬁnite tensor products of Hilbert spaces and crossed product
constructions.
• Type III factors: A von Neumann algebra is a Type III factor if it has no
minimal projections and no projections equivalent to the identity. Type
III factors can be further divided into subtypes based on their modular
theory properties. Although very abstract, type III factors play a crucial
role in extended quantum thermodynamical systems and in algebraic
quantum ﬁeld theory [11, 25].
The von Neumann-Murray decomposition theorem serves as a crucial tool
for breaking down a general von Neumann algebra into a direct integral of

2.3.4 Superselection rules
The entire framework of von Neumann algebras (and C∗-algebras) is built on
the concept of modeling quantum observables. However, in reality, not all
self-adjoint operators can be considered as observables.
A well-known example is the electrical charge observable, which commutes
with all other observables. As a result, since all quantum systems possess
electrical charges, any physically viable observable must be able to commute
with the electrical charge observable. Self-adjoint operators that do not com-
mute with electrical charges cannot be physical. This constraint on physically
permissible observables (and therefore states) is known as a superselection
rule.
There are additional superselection rules in physics, though they vary greatly
in nature, such as integer/semi-integer angular momenta and Bargmann’s
superselection rule on mass in non-relativistic physics.

In an effort to ﬁnd a linear hierarchy equivalent to the factorisation
