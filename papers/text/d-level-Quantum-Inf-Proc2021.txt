Abstract
In this paper, we propose a secure multi-party quantum modulo- d summation proto-
col using d-level entanglement swapping, where a malicious but non-collusive third
party (TP) who plays a role of entanglement distribution between the participants
is involved. TP is assumed to be malicious but non-collusive, which means TP can
launch various attacks within boundaries of quantum mechanics except the collusion
with any dishonest participant. Our protocol is congenitally free from Trojan horse
attacks because there is no need to relay encoded states. In addition, only d-level Bell
measurement is required for the users except TP , which makes our protocol’s imple-
mentation more feasible. We show that the presented protocol is secure against outside
as well as participant attacks. Second, we give a detailed comparison of different quan-
tum modulo-d summation protocols, in terms of qudit cost, quantum resources, and
quantum operations. In the end, we discuss how to replace cat states with d-level
Bell states in Je et al.’s protocols (Quantum Inf Process 18(6):168, 2019) for easier
implementation with current quantum technology.
Keywords Quantum multi-party computation · Quantum summation · Entanglement
swapping

to quantum voting [ 11–15], quantum anonymous ranking [ 16], and quantum private

A variety of quantum summation protocols have been investigated by using various
quantum resources. Reference [20] presented a quantum summation protocol based on
single photons in both polarization and spatial-mode degrees of freedom, where unitary
operations employed to encode users’ private bits on the travelling single photons are
required. Most other protocols depend on sharing a multipartite entangled state among
users. For example, Zhang et al. [21] designed a quantum summation protocol without
a trusted third party, in which the number of users was limited to three because of the use
of the so-called genuinely maximally entangled six-qubit states. In 2016, Shi et al. [22]
proposed protocols for summation and multiplication that involve quantum Fourier
transform, controlled-NOT (CNOT) gates and oracle operators. They then studied
a common quantum solution that addresses a class of two-party private summation
problems [23]. In 2017, Zhang et al. [ 24] devised a multi-party quantum summation
protocol without a trusted third party where a multipartite entangled state is shared
among users. Liu et al. [ 25] presented a quantum summation protocol by sharing
multipartite entangled states that include Bell states as subsystems among users. In
their protocol, quantum communication is two-way, which means they should offer
special care concerning Trojan horse attacks [ 26–28]. In contrast to these protocols,
Zhang et al. [ 29] employed quantum teleportation to devise a quantum summation
protocol, in which a certain entanglement distribution protocol is involved.
Recently, Ji et al. [ 30] discussed how to construct quantum private comparison,
quantum anonymous ranking, and quantum anonymous voting protocols based on
quantum summation. They designed two quantum summation protocols by employing
entanglement swapping property between the d-level Bell state and the d-level n-
partite cat state. As the d-level n-partite cat state remains difﬁcult to prepare with the
current technology, we try to replace them with d-level Bell states.
In some quantum cryptographical protocols, a semi-honest third party (TP) is usu-
ally involved to help compute some results [ 5,20]. TP is said to be semi-honest if TP
executes the protocols loyally, writes down a record of all its intermediate computa-
tions, and might try to steal the participants’ private inputs from the record, but he
cannot collude with other participants. In our protocol, a malicious but non-collusive
TP is introduced to help the computation. TP is said to be malicious but non-collusive
if he can do whatever he wants within boundaries of quantum mechanics except the
collusion with dishonest participants. The difference between the semi-honest TP and
the TP in our protocol is that the TP in our protocol does not need to execute the
protocol loyally; that is to say, the malicious TP has more possible ways to attack the
protocol, which in return makes the protocol more secure. For this point, we give the
assumption of the malicious but non-collusive TP .
In this work, we start with a quantum modulo- d protocol relying on the entan-
glement swapping property between d-level Bell states in which the malicious but
non-collusive TP is introduced. A security analysis shows that our protocol can resist
both outside and participant attacks. We then compare in detail our protocol with exist-

3 The proposed protocol
We are now ready to describe our protocol satisfying the following requirements:
(1) Correctness the modulo-d summation of all users’ private inputs should be correct.
(2) Security an outside eavesdropper cannot obtain any information about each user’s
private input.
(3) Privacy no user can learn about other users’ private inputs.
(4) Fairness All users get the summation result at the same time.
The classical and quantum channels used in our protocol are assumed to be authen-
ticated and noiseless, respectively. The third party (TP) who distributes d-level Bell
states between users and computes the modulo-d summation of all users’ private inputs
is assumed to be malicious but non-collusive; that is to say, TP is allowed to launch
various attacks by using different quantum resources except the collusion with any
dishonest user. For simplicity, TP will be donated by P1 in the remainder of the paper.

4.2 Security analysis
Let us now move on to the security analysis. We have to show that the proposed
protocol can resist both outside and participant attacks, and it thus satisﬁes the security,
privacy and fairness requirements mentioned in Sect.3. Outside attacks may potentially
affect our protocol via the initial entanglement distribution. Note that P1, as a special
participant, cannot collude with any dishonest user, but he can launch attacks within
boundaries of quantum mechanics. Other dishonest users may collude to steal the rest
users’ private inputs. We will show how we can detect these attacks by using decoy
states and extra d-level Bell states. Trojan horse attacks [28,32,33], such as the delay-
photon Trojan horse attacks as well as the invisible photon eavesdropping Trojan horse
attack, usually exist in quantum communication protocols where quantum states are
encoded and relayed. As the entanglement distribution occurs only once in a one-way
communication, these attacks become invalid to our protocol.
4.2.1 Analysis of outside attacks
We now give a detailed analysis of the security of our protocol against outside
attacks. The decoy-state technique is utilized in our protocol. The idea comes from
the ﬁrst quantum key distribution protocol by Bennett and Brassard [ 34] that has been

entanglement-measurement attack, and the denial-of-service (DOS) attack. Without
loss of generality, the most general strategy that the outside attacker, Eve, can adopt
is that she uses the operation UE to entangle the quantum state transmitted from P1 to
Pj with her ancillary quantum system |E⟩, which can be characterized as

4.2.2 Analysis of participant attacks
Let us now study the security of our protocol against participant attacks. We need to
account for two kinds of attacks: one from P1 who distributes the d-level Bell states
between users, and the other from dishonest users.
Analysis of Attacks from P 1. We ﬁrst analyse the attacks from P1, who cannot
collude with any dishonest user. Without loss generality, suppose that P1 attempts to
obtain Pk ’ private inputs ( mk1, mk2)( k ⁄= 2, n + 1). To achieve this, P1 may carry
out the entanglement swapping attack as follows: P1 ﬁrst distributes two d-level Bell
states shared between him and Pk . P1 can then derive Pk ’s private key (vk,w k) using
Eq. ( 1), because he knows the initial d-level states and his own measurement result
in the d-level Bell basis; P1 can thus obtain Pk ’s private inputs. Another attack is that
P1 distributes a d-level Bell state to Pk without sharing it with other user. It is easy
for P1 to obtain Pk ’s measurement result, and, therefore, to get Pk ’s private inputs.

5 Comparison and discussion
In this section, we ﬁrst compare our protocol with existing quantum modulo- d sum-
mation protocols in terms of qudit cost, quantum resource, and quantum operations.
And then we discuss how to replace the cat states in the protocols of Ref. [ 30] with
the d-level Bell states, which facilitates the protocol’s implementation.
The qudit cost of a quantum summation protocol is deﬁned as the number of qudits
(including the qudits used for security detection) required to obtain a modulo- d sum-
mation of n private dits, where n is the number of users.
For simplicity, we assume that all protocols use n decoy qudits to check eavesdrop-
ping whenever necessary.
Shi et al. [22] utilized qubits to construct modulo-d summation protocols, in which
d = 2m and m is the number of qubits. For comparison, we regardd qubits as a qudit. As
the authors mentioned, their protocol I can only protect the private input of the initiator,
who starts the protocol. To overcome this weakness, they proposed protocol III based
on protocol I, which can be secure against attacks from any less n − 1 dishonest users.
To compute the modulo-d summation of n private dits provided byn users, protocol III
should call protocol I n + 1 times, with each time, protocol I consuming n + 1 qudits.
Totally, protocol III costs (n + 1)(n + 1) = n2 + 2n + 1 qudits. Their protocol needs
d-level ( n + 1)-partite entangled d-level states and quantum operations, including
the (inverse) quantum Fourier transform, the controlled-NOT (CNOT) operation, an
oracle operation, and single qubit measurement. Protocols II and IV in their work are
about modulo-d multiplication, which are not discussed here.
Y ang et al. [ 36] presented a secure multi-party summation protocol based on d-
level n-partite entangled states, where n is the number of users. A d-level n-partite
entangled state is required to obtain a modulo- d summation of n private dits. Besides,
n(n − 1) decoy qudits are needed to prevent outside attacks, and (n − 1) d-level n-
partite entangled states should be employed to detect the initiator’s honesty. The qudit
cost thus becomes n + n(n − 1)+ n(n − 1) = 2n2 − n. In their protocol, the quantum
Fourier transform and unitary operations related to users’ private inputs are necessary.
The single qudit measurements are performed to read the summation result.
In our previous work [37], d-level single quantum systems are employed to design
a multi-party quantum summation protocol, whered is limited to a prime integer. Only
one d-level single state is needed to compute a dit sum. However, it needs relaying
n + 1 times and each time, n decoy qudits are required to detect eavesdropping. The
qudit cost is thus 1+(n+1)n = n2 +n+1. The protocol needs two unitary operations
to combine to transform an element in a d-level basis to another element in another

basis. Besides, each participant should be equipped with quantum state generators and
the measurement devices in d + 1 different bases.
Ji et al.’s [ 30] protocol I is probabilistic can easily be modiﬁed into a modulo- d
summation protocol. The only change is the last step, where the participant, who helps
the computation, can just perform the summation in modulo d instead of the general
one. The protocol then becomes deterministic. In this case, their protocol I needs a
d-level (n + 1)-partite cat state and nd -level Bell states. Besides, TP and n users
have to transmit particles to each other, which requires 2 n2 qudits for eavesdropping
detection. Hence, their protocol’s qudit cost is (n + 1) + 2n + 2n2 = 2n2 + 3n + 1.
In addition, their protocols need the measurement in the n-partite cat basis, which is
not available when n is very large with current quantum technology. Their protocol II
based on protocol I aims at solving other problems, which is omitted here.
In our protocol in this work, we take advantage of the entanglement swapping
property of d-level Bell states to devise a quantum modulo- d summation protocol,
where a two-dit summation of n users’ private inputs, each with two dits, is computed.
n + 1 pairs of d-level Bell states are required for the computation, and n2 decoy qudits
are used for outside eavesdropping detection. Also, n2 pairs d-level Bell states are
utilized to check the initiator’s honesty. The qudit cost is, therefore, ((n + 1) + 2n +
n2 + 2n2)/2 = (3n2 + 3n + 1)/2. The quantum operations users need include only
the d-level Bell measurement and the single qudit measurement.

6 Conclusions
We ﬁrst designed a multi-party quantum modulo- d summation by taking advantage
of the entanglement swapping property of d-level Bell states. A malicious but non-
collusive third party (TP), also known as P1 in our protocol, plays a role of distributing
entanglement shared between users in such a way that he can ﬁnally obtain the sum-
mation result according to n users’ cipher-texts. The correctness and the security of
the proposed protocol were analysed in detail. Afterward, we gave a comparison of
